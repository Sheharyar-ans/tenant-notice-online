California AB 1482 Tenant Protection Act: Just Cause Evictions and Rent Caps

Under California Civil Code Section 1946.2, residential landlords cannot terminate tenancies occupied for twelve months or longer without established at-fault or no-fault just cause. No-fault terminations require one month of relocation assistance paid within 15 days, and annual rent increases cannot exceed 5 percent plus CPI under Section 1947.12.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

The California Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (AB 1482), codified in California Civil Code Sections 1946.2 and 1947.12, establishes statewide rent caps and just cause eviction rules. Prior to this legislation, landlords outside municipal rent control areas could terminate periodic tenancies on standard 30-day or 60-day notices without cause. Under Civil Code Section 1946.2, once any tenant continuously occupies a dwelling for twelve months or more, a landlord cannot terminate the tenancy without demonstrating an authorized at-fault or no-fault just cause.

The statute operates in tandem with Civil Code Section 1947.12, which imposes a strict annual rent increase ceiling. The maximum allowable rent adjustment is limited to 5 percent plus the percentage change in the regional Consumer Price Index (CPI), or 10 percent, whichever calculation yields the lower figure. Landlords may not enact more than two gross rent increases within any 12-month period, and the cumulative adjustment across those installments cannot breach the statutory cap. The rent limitation applies automatically unless the residential real property falls into a clearly defined statutory category of exemption.

Landlords drafting notices must differentiate between properties subject to the act and properties claiming statutory exemption. Under Civil Code Section 1946.2(e), exemptions include single-family homes and condominiums where the owner is not a real estate investment trust (REIT), corporation, or limited liability company with corporate members, provided that the tenant was served with explicit statutory disclosure language in the initial lease. Newly constructed residential properties possessing a certificate of occupancy issued within the preceding 15 years, transient hotels, owner-occupied duplexes where the owner resided on the premises prior to the tenancy, and dormitories are likewise exempt from both the rent cap and just cause provisions.

In instances where unpaid rent arises alongside just cause disputes, landlords must also review the strict timing rules of a California 3-day notice to pay rent or quit to prevent conflicting legal claims in court.

Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

Serving a termination notice under California Civil Code Section 1946.2 requires compliance with California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1162 and Section 12. For at-fault evictions based on curable lease violations, such as unauthorized pets or unapproved occupants, the landlord must first serve a 3-day notice to cure or quit. The computation of this 3-day cure window strictly excludes the day of delivery, Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2). If the tenant cures the breach within the statutory 3-day timeframe, the landlord cannot proceed to terminate the tenancy.

In no-fault just cause proceedings, the landlord must provide a full 60-day written notice to vacate if the tenant has resided in the unit for twelve months or longer. Day one of the sixty-day countdown commences on the calendar day directly following confirmed service. If notice is served via posting and mailing under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1162(3), or through certified mail, the statutory mailing rules under Section 1013 add five calendar days for domestic California addresses, extending the mandatory notice horizon to 65 days.

The provision of statutory relocation assistance constitutes a jurisdictional condition precedent under Civil Code Section 1946.2(d). The landlord must elect between providing a direct relocation payment equal to one month of the tenant's current contract rent, or waiving in writing the payment of the final month's rent prior to it becoming due. If the landlord elects the monetary payment, the funds must be delivered to the tenant within 15 calendar days of serving the termination notice. Failure to tender the exact relocation payment within this 15-day statutory window invalidates the notice to vacate completely.

Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The primary fatal drafting error under California AB 1482 is omitting mandatory statutory exemption language in the residential lease agreement. Under Civil Code Section 1946.2(e)(8)(B), an owner of an exempt single-family home or condo cannot claim exemption unless the lease contains specific statutory text notifying the tenant of the exemption. Without this written disclosure, the tenancy is legally deemed covered by the act, rendering any non-cause notice void.

A second fatal defect involves unsupported substantial remodel notices under Senate Bill 567. A landlord claiming no-fault eviction for substantial remodel must obtain all municipal building permits prior to serving notice, attach copies to the notice itself, and provide a detailed written explanation of why the repairs require the tenant to vacate for at least 30 continuous days. Serving a vague remodel notice without permits triggers civil liability and invalidates the notice.

A third fatal mistake is failing to deliver the mandatory relocation payment within the strict 15-day statutory window. If a landlord serves a no-fault 60-day notice but tenders the one-month relocation check on day 16 or at move-out, the notice is legally void on its face. The tenant may successfully defend against the unlawful detainer lawsuit, and the court will award mandatory attorney fees to the tenant under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1021.5.

A fourth fatal defect is confusing at-fault incurable grounds with curable grounds. If a landlord serves an unconditional 3-day notice to quit for a curable lease violation, such as unauthorized pets or paying late fees, the eviction action will be dismissed. Landlords must provide the statutory notice to cure prior to issuing an incurable termination notice.

Finally, accepting rent after the expiration of a just cause notice without an express written non-waiver agreement forfeits termination rights. Accepting payment for occupancy beyond the notice termination date creates a new periodic tenancy, requiring the landlord to commence the entire statutory notice sequence anew.

Frequently Asked Questions

Under California Civil Code Section 1946.2, residential tenancies where any tenant has continuously occupied the dwelling for twelve months or more are protected by mandatory just cause eviction rules, unless an explicit statutory exemption applies.

California Civil Code Section 1947.12 caps annual rent increases at 5 percent plus the regional Consumer Price Index (CPI), or 10 percent total, whichever is lower, with a maximum of two installments permitted across any 12-month period.

Under California Civil Code Section 1946.2(d), landlords issuing a no-fault just cause eviction notice must provide one month of rent as relocation assistance within 15 calendar days of notice service, or waive the final month rent in writing.

Single-family homes and condominiums are exempt only if the owner is not a corporation or REIT, and the tenant was provided written statutory disclosure of the exemption directly within the signed lease agreement pursuant to Section 1946.2(e)(8).

Under California Civil Code Section 1946.2(d)(4), failure to deliver the statutory relocation payment within 15 calendar days of notice service renders the notice to vacate completely void, requiring dismissal of any subsequent unlawful detainer lawsuit.
