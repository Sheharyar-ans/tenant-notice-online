Renters Rights Act Complete Guide: Section 21 Abolition and Periodic Tenancy Rules

The Renters Rights Act permanently abolishes Section 21 no-fault evictions in England and converts all assured shorthold tenancies into open-ended periodic contracts. Landlords can only regain possession by proving statutory Section 8 grounds in County Court, and tenants receive a statutory two-month notice to quit right.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

The Renters' Rights Act represents the most profound statutory restructuring of English residential tenancy law since the passage of the Housing Act 1988. At the core of this legislative overhaul is the permanent abolition of Section 21 of the Housing Act 1988, widely characterized in legal commentary as no-fault eviction. Prior to this statutory transformation, private landlords in England and Wales could terminate an Assured Shorthold Tenancy (AST) at the conclusion of a fixed term by serving a two-month notice without asserting any legal ground, breach of contract, or statutory justification. Under the reformed statutory regime, all existing and future residential tenancies are converted into rolling periodic tenancies.

With the repeal of Section 21, private landlords can no longer regain possession of a residential dwelling through summary or accelerated possession procedures. Possession can only be obtained by proving one or more statutory grounds enumerated within Schedule 2 of the Housing Act 1988. Landlords seeking possession must serve a formal Section 8 notice on Form 3, citing specific mandatory or discretionary grounds and submitting persuasive documentary evidence to the County Court. The abolition of fixed-term contracts fundamentally alters the tenant rights landscape. Under the periodic tenancy framework, tenants are no longer locked into 12-month or 24-month fixed terms with onerous break clauses. Instead, tenants hold ongoing statutory security of tenure and may terminate their tenancy at any point by serving a compliant two-month notice to quit. In addition, the Renters' Rights Act establishes robust protections against backdoor evictions orchestrated through excessive economic demands. Landlords can no longer impose unilateral rent adjustments through rent review clauses buried in tenancy contracts. All annual rent adjustments must proceed exclusively through the statutory Section 13 procedure, affording tenants the unimpeded legal right to challenge unreasonable increases before the First-tier Tribunal. Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

Under the reformed Housing Act 1988 framework, the calculation of statutory notice horizons for Section 8 notices is governed strictly by the civil procedure rules of England and Wales. When serving a Section 8 notice on Form 3, the date on which the notice is delivered to or received by the tenant is day zero and cannot be counted toward the statutory notice window. The statutory notice countdown commences on the day immediately following confirmed service. If a mandatory ground requires a four-week or four-month notice horizon, that complete temporal period must expire before a landlord can issue a claim form in the County Court.

Lawful service of statutory notices in England is governed by Section 196 of the Law of Property Act 1925, subject to explicit provisions within the written tenancy agreement. Permissible mechanisms include personal in-hand delivery to the tenant, leaving the notice at the tenant's last known place of abode, or sending via recorded delivery postal service. If served via recorded post, service is legally deemed effected on the second business day following dispatch, excluding weekends and public bank holidays.

For landlords seeking possession under newly enacted grounds, such as Ground 1A for property sale or Ground 1 for personal occupation, a mandatory four-month notice horizon applies. Crucially, these owner-occupation and marketing grounds cannot be utilized during the initial 12-month protected tenancy period. Landlords cannot serve notice under Ground 1A until the tenant has occupied the dwelling for at least eight continuous months, ensuring the four-month notice expires no earlier than the first anniversary of the tenancy commencement date.

Tenants ending their tenancy under the reformed system must serve at least two months written notice. The notice does not need to expire on a specific rental period boundary unless contractually stipulated, but must clearly identify the intended vacate date. Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The most severe fatal mistake is serving a legacy Section 21 Form 6A notice after the statutory abolition date. A Section 21 notice served under the reformed regime is completely void and carries zero legal effect. Any possession claim issued on the basis of a Section 21 notice will be struck out immediately by the County Court District Judge, with adverse costs awarded against the landlord.

A second fatal drafting trap is utilizing an outdated version of Form 3 when serving a Section 8 notice. The Renters' Rights Act introduced amended prescribed text for Form 3, incorporating revised statutory grounds and expanded tenant advice notices. Serving an obsolete version of Form 3 that omits mandatory statutory warnings invalidates the proceedings, preventing the court from granting a possession order under Section 8(2) of the Housing Act 1988.

A third critical error is attempting to invoke Ground 1A (intention to sell) or Ground 1 (landlord move-in) during the initial 12-month protected tenancy period. The statute creates an absolute bar against these grounds taking effect during the first year of occupancy. Serving a notice that purports to terminate the tenancy within the first 12 months is legally defective, and the County Court has no statutory jurisdiction to waive this requirement.

A fourth fatal defect is miscalculating notice periods for rent arrears under reformed Ground 8. Under the amended statutory criteria, Ground 8 requires at least three months of unpaid rent (or 13 weeks for weekly tenancies) both at the date of notice service and at the date of the court hearing, with a mandatory four-week notice period. Issuing a notice based on the historic two-month standard will result in dismissal of the possession claim.

Finally, failure to protect the tenant deposit within an approved government scheme (DPS, TDS, or MyDeposits) within 30 days of receipt bars landlords from relying on certain possession grounds and exposes the owner to statutory penalties of one to three times the deposit value under Section 214 of the Housing Act 2004.
