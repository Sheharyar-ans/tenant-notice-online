California 30-Day vs 60-Day Notice to Vacate: Statutory Rules and Day Calculations

Under California Civil Code Section 1946.1, a landlord terminating a periodic residential tenancy must serve at least 30 days written notice if the tenant has occupied the dwelling for less than one year, or 60 days if occupancy is one year or longer. The day of service is excluded from statutory calculations.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

Residential lease terminations in the State of California are governed primarily by California Civil Code Section 1946.1. This statute establishes an uncompromising procedural framework that differentiates between short-term occupancies and long-term residential tenancies. When a landlord or tenant seeks to terminate a periodic tenancy, such as a month-to-month agreement, the law imposes strict minimum notice horizons that cannot be shortened by informal agreement. Utilizing a compliant California 30-day notice to vacate template ensures that landlords and letting agents satisfy these rigid judicial benchmarks prior to filing an unlawful detainer lawsuit.

Under Civil Code Section 1946.1(b), an owner of a residential dwelling must give at least 60 days written notice to terminate a tenancy if any tenant or resident has resided in the dwelling for one year or more. Conversely, under Section 1946.1(c), an owner may serve an abbreviated 30 days written notice only if all tenants and residents have resided in the residential unit for less than one continuous year. This tenure distinction reflects the legislative intent to provide long-standing residents with sufficient temporal runway to secure replacement housing in competitive rental markets.

A narrow statutory exception exists under Civil Code Section 1946.1(d). A landlord may serve a 30-day notice on a tenant who has resided in the property for one year or more only if four cumulative criteria are satisfied: the dwelling is an alienable single-family home or condominium, the owner has entered into a bona fide contract to sell the unit to a natural person purchaser, an escrow has been established with a licensed escrow agent, and the notice is served within 120 days of opening escrow. If any of these four statutory conditions fail, the landlord must provide the full 60-day notice window.

Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

Calculating the exact effective date of a California termination notice requires strict adherence to California Code of Civil Procedure Section 12. Under this procedural rule, the date on which the notice is personally handed to or received by the tenant is entirely excluded from the computation. Day one of the statutory countdown begins on the calendar day immediately following service. For instance, if a 30-day notice is served on October 1, day one is October 2, making October 31 the earliest permissible vacate date.

California Code of Civil Procedure Section 12a governs deadlines that expire on weekends or official court holidays. If the thirtieth or sixtieth day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or judicial holiday, the tenant is granted by operation of law until the end of the next judicial business day to surrender possession. Landlords who file an unlawful detainer complaint on a Monday morning following a Sunday expiration date commit a jurisdictional error, as the tenant possessed the entire Monday business day to surrender keys.

Service must be completed using one of the three statutory methods codified in California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1162: personal in-hand delivery to the tenant, substituted service upon a person of suitable age and discretion at the tenant residence or place of business followed by first class mailing, or conspicuous posting on the property coupled with immediate first class mailing. When service is executed via substituted service or posting, landlords should cross-reference our California 24-hour notice to enter guidelines to ensure lawful access onto the curtilage.

In addition, service by mail invokes California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1013. Where a notice to vacate is served exclusively by mail, five additional calendar days must be added to the statutory notice window if the mailing address is within the State of California. Consequently, a mailed 30-day notice requires 35 clear days, while a mailed 60-day notice requires 65 days before an eviction lawsuit can be lawfully instituted in Superior Court.

Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The most prevalent fatal drafting error committed by California property owners is serving an unauthorized 30-day notice on a tenant whose occupancy crossed the 365-day threshold. In calculating tenure, California courts count continuous residential occupancy, including prior fixed-term leases that rolled over into month-to-month tenancies. If a tenant initially signed a six-month lease and subsequently remained for an additional seven months on a periodic basis, total tenure equals thirteen months, mandating a 60-day notice.

The second critical drafting trap involves the California Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (AB 1482), codified at California Civil Code Section 1946.2. For residential tenancies subject to AB 1482 where any tenant has continuously occupied the property for twelve months or more, a landlord cannot terminate the tenancy without legitimate just cause. Serving a standard no-fault 60-day notice without stating the statutory just cause ground, or failing to offer the mandatory one-month relocation assistance payment pursuant to Section 1946.2(d), renders the notice void on its face.

Third, landlords frequently violate California Civil Code Section 1942.5, which prohibits retaliatory evictions. A notice to vacate served within 180 days of a tenant exercising statutory rights, such as reporting habitability violations to local code enforcement, requesting repairs, or organizing a tenant association, creates a legal presumption of retaliation. A tenant establishing retaliation can recover statutory damages up to two thousand dollars per violation alongside actual damages and attorney fees.

Finally, premature filing of an unlawful detainer action represents an incurable defect. If a landlord files an eviction lawsuit in California Superior Court even one day prior to the complete expiration of the notice horizon, the complaint must be dismissed upon demurrer or motion for summary judgment. The landlord cannot amend the complaint to cure premature filing; rather, the landlord must serve an entirely new notice and begin the statutory waiting period anew.
