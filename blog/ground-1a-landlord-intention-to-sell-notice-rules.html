Ground 1A Landlord Intention to Sell: Statutory Notice Rules and Timelines

Ground 1A of Schedule 2 of the Housing Act 1988 allows landlords to recover possession to sell the property by serving four months written notice. The notice cannot take effect during the first 12 months of tenancy, and landlords face fines up to £40,000 for re-letting within 12 months.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

With the statutory abolition of Section 21 no-fault evictions, the Renters' Rights Act introduced specific possession grounds within Schedule 2 of the Housing Act 1988 to permit private landlords to recover property when genuine commercial circumstances require liquidation. Prominent among these newly enacted provisions is Ground 1A, which establishes a mandatory possession ground where a landlord intends to sell the residential dwelling. Ground 1A is subject to rigorous statutory safeguards designed to prevent landlords from manufacturing false intentions to circumvent tenancy security rules. First, Ground 1A requires a mandatory notice period of four full calendar months. Landlords cannot initiate possession proceedings in County Court until this four-month statutory notice horizon has elapsed in its entirety. Second, the statute imposes an absolute 12-month protected tenancy period. Ground 1A cannot be used to terminate a tenancy during the initial twelve months from the commencement date of the tenancy.

In addition, the statute imposes severe re-letting and marketing restrictions following the recovery of possession under Ground 1A. A landlord who obtains possession of a dwelling under Ground 1A is statutorily prohibited from marketing the property for rent or re-letting the dwelling on a new tenancy for a period of twelve months from the date possession is surrendered. Local housing authorities are empowered to issue financial penalties of up to £7,000 for initial breaches, and up to £40,000 or criminal prosecution for repeat offenses.

Tenants served with a Ground 1A notice possess the statutory right to serve a counter-notice or terminate early by giving two months written notice under reformed periodic tenancy rules. Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

The calculation of the four-month notice horizon under Ground 1A is strictly governed by the civil procedure rules of England and Wales. The day on which Form 3 is served is excluded from the calculation. Day one commences on the day immediately following service. A four-month notice served on October 15 must specify a date no earlier than February 16 after which court proceedings can begin. If the four-month period ends on a weekend or public bank holiday, court proceedings cannot be issued until the next working day.

A key statutory interaction exists between the four-month notice period and the 12-month protected tenancy buffer. A landlord cannot serve a notice under Ground 1A that takes effect before the end of the first twelve months of the tenancy. Therefore, if a tenancy commenced on January 1, the earliest date a Ground 1A notice can take effect is January 1 of the following year. Serving a four-month notice on May 1 that purports to expire on September 1 of year one is void as a matter of law. The earliest date a landlord could serve a four-month notice is September 1 of the first year, expiring after the 12-month buffer ends.

Service must be completed using statutory methods under Section 196 of the Law of Property Act 1925. Permissible methods include personal hand delivery to the tenant, leaving the notice at the property, or recorded post. For postal service, CPR 6.26 deems service on the second business day after posting, meaning landlords must add two working days to their four-month calculation to ensure the full statutory period is respected.

Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The most prevalent fatal mistake under Ground 1A is attempting to terminate a tenancy during the initial 12-month protected tenancy window. If a landlord serves a Ground 1A notice six months into a tenancy, the notice is invalid on its face. The County Court has no legal power to grant possession where a notice breaches the statutory 12-month protection buffer, and the claim will be dismissed with costs awarded against the landlord.

The second critical drafting defect is failing to provide the full four-month statutory notice period. Landlords who miscalculate calendar months or fail to account for the two days of deemed service for postal dispatch risk issuing claims prematurely. If a notice falls short by even one day, the County Court will strike out the possession claim.

A third fatal mistake is lacking genuine documentary proof of intention to sell. Ground 1A requires the landlord to prove a bona fide commercial intention to sell the freehold or long leasehold interest in the property. At court, the landlord must provide concrete evidence such as a signed estate agent marketing agreement, solicitor correspondence, or instruction documents. If the court suspects the claim is pretextual, possession will be refused.

A fourth fatal defect is re-letting or advertising the property for rent following possession. Landlords who obtain possession under Ground 1A and subsequently re-let the property within twelve months face substantial financial penalties from local councils under the Renters' Rights Act civil penalty framework, with fines up to £7,000 for initial offenses and up to £40,000 for serious violations.

Finally, failure to utilize the updated prescribed Form 3 containing the specific statutory wording for Ground 1A will render the notice invalid. Landlords cannot adapt old Section 8 forms or draft custom letters. Prescribed statutory text must be strictly adhered to under English tenancy law.
