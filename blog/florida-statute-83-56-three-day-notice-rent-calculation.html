Florida Statute 83.56: Three-Day Notice Rent Calculation and Delivery Rules

Florida Statute Section 83.56(3) mandates that a three-day notice to pay rent or vacate must exclude the day of delivery, Saturdays, Sundays, and legal court holidays. Demands must be restricted to base contract rent unless the signed lease defines fees as additional rent, and mailed notices require five extra days.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

Under the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, codified in Chapter 83 of the Florida Statutes, evictions for nonpayment of rent are governed strictly by Florida Statute Section 83.56(3). This statute establishes that a landlord cannot institute summary eviction proceedings in Florida County Court without first serving the tenant with a written demand requiring either the payment of rent or the surrender of possession within three days. Utilizing a verified Florida 3-day notice to pay rent or quit is essential to satisfy the exacting judicial demands of Florida county judges.

The defining feature of Florida Statute Section 83.56(3) is its mandatory day-counting exclusion. The statute explicitly commands that the three-day notice horizon must exclude the date of delivery, Saturdays, Sundays, and legal court holidays recognized by the county in which the rental property is located. Landlords frequently miscalculate this timeframe by treating the notice period as seventy-two consecutive chronological hours. In Florida jurisprudence, an eviction complaint based upon a notice that fails to provide three complete judicial business days is jurisdictionally fatal, requiring immediate dismissal with prejudice to that specific notice.

In addition, Florida law strictly restricts the financial claims that may be included within a statutory 3-day notice. Section 83.56(3) permits demands solely for rent past due. Florida courts have consistently ruled that late fees, utility surcharges, pet fees, or repair costs cannot be included in a three-day notice unless the signed residential lease agreement contains an explicit clause defining those incidental charges as additional rent. Demanding non-rent amounts in a 3-day notice renders the entire statutory demand defective under binding Florida appellate precedent.

If a lease termination involves general tenure expiration rather than monetary default, Florida Statute Section 83.57 governs, requiring fifteen days written notice for month-to-month tenancies. In all cases, strict statutory language must be mirrored precisely to avoid procedural challenges during judicial hearings.

Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

Calculating the three-day deadline under Florida law requires meticulous adherence to Florida Rules of General Practice and Judicial Administration Rule 2.514. The day the notice is handed to the tenant or posted at the residence is day zero and is never counted. Day one begins on the next business day that is not a weekend or court holiday. For example, if a landlord posts a notice on a Thursday, Friday is day one. Saturday and Sunday are excluded by statutory mandate. Monday is day two, and Tuesday is day three. The tenant has until midnight on Tuesday to tender payment. The landlord cannot file an eviction complaint in County Court until Wednesday morning.

Legal holidays under Florida Statute Section 83.56(3) include all official state holidays recognized under Florida Statute Section 110.117 as well as local judicial holidays declared by the chief judge of the local circuit court. If the county clerk of court is closed for Good Friday, the day after Thanksgiving, or emergency weather closures, that closure day cannot be counted as one of the three statutory days. Landlords must verify local court calendars prior to calculating notice expiration.

Florida Statute Section 83.56(4) governs delivery methods. The notice must be delivered by mail, personal in-hand delivery to the tenant, or by leaving a copy at the residence if the tenant is absent from the dwelling. If the landlord chooses to serve the notice by mail, Florida Rule of General Practice 2.514(b) applies, requiring the landlord to add five additional calendar days to the three-day notice period. A mailed 3-day notice therefore requires a minimum of eight business days before an eviction suit can be lawfully commenced.

The notice must specify the exact name, physical street address, and telephone number of the landlord or authorized agent entitled to receive the rent. If payment is required by mail, the notice must explicitly state so and allow sufficient additional postal time. If a physical address is designated, the landlord must ensure that payment can be tendered at that location during reasonable business hours throughout the entire statutory notice window.

Tenants departing the property should also understand their rights concerning tenancy funds. Under Florida Statute Section 83.49, landlords have fifteen days to return a security deposit if no damages are claimed, or thirty days to make a claim via certified mail. Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The single most common fatal drafting error under Florida Statute Section 83.56(3) is counting weekends or court holidays. Serving a notice on Wednesday with payment demanded by Saturday is a jurisdictional error. An eviction complaint predicated on a notice containing an incorrect deadline will be dismissed, with mandatory attorney fees assessed against the landlord under Section 83.48.

The second critical fatal defect is commingling late fees or utilities with overdue rent on the face of the notice. Unless the written lease explicitly defines late charges and utilities as additional rent, including them invalidates the notice. Florida judges will strike down the entire notice as an improper statutory demand.

A third fatal mistake is failing to provide a physical payment location. If a Florida 3-day notice lists only a PO Box without adding five days for postal transit, or fails to list a contact telephone number, the notice violates the statutory template. Florida courts demand strict compliance with statutory form requirements.

A fourth fatal defect is premature filing of the eviction complaint in County Court. Filing on day three prior to midnight deprives the court of jurisdiction because the tenant maintained the right to cure throughout that business day. Landlords must wait until the full three business days have elapsed.

Finally, accepting partial rent payments during the pendency of a 3-day notice waives the landlord right to terminate the tenancy under Florida Statute Section 83.56(5), unless the landlord provides a written receipt stating that acceptance does not waive the notice.
