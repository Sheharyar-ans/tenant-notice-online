Texas Property Code 24.005: Notice to Vacate Procedure and Statutory Deadlines

Under Texas Property Code Section 24.005, a landlord must provide at least three calendar days written notice to vacate before filing a forcible detainer suit, unless a written lease specifies a different timeframe. Notice must be delivered personally, affixed to the inside of the door, or sent via certified mail.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

Evictions in Texas are governed by Chapter 24 of the Texas Property Code. Texas Property Code Section 24.005 establishes mandatory notice requirements that landlords must satisfy prior to filing a forcible detainer suit in a Texas Justice of the Peace Court. A landlord cannot file an eviction suit until the tenant has been properly served with a written notice to vacate and the statutory notice period has elapsed. Using an authorized Texas notice to vacate template avoids procedural dismissals in municipal Justice Courts.

Texas Property Code Section 24.005(a) requires at least three calendar days written notice to vacate before filing for eviction, unless a written lease contract specifies a different period. Texas courts enforce written lease provisions strictly: if a signed lease specifies 24 hours notice, that contractual term supersedes the statutory three-day default. If the lease is silent or oral, the three-day rule applies without exception.

The content of a Texas notice to vacate must be absolute and unequivocal. Unless the lease requires an opportunity to cure, a Texas notice to vacate demands unconditional surrender of possession by a date certain. Statements suggesting the tenant may remain upon partial payment can render the demand legally defective in Justice Court.

Under the federal CARES Act, properties backed by federal loans (such as Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, or VA) remain subject to a mandatory 30-day notice requirement for nonpayment of rent. Landlords with covered properties cannot rely on the Texas three-day rule. When dealing with delinquent rent, review our Texas 3-day notice to pay rent or quit for statutory compliance.

Legal Service of Process & Statutory Day Calculation

Calculating the exact expiration date for a Texas notice to vacate is governed by Texas Rules of Civil Procedure Rule 500.5. Under this rule, the calendar day on which the notice is delivered or posted is completely excluded from the computation. Day one of the statutory waiting period begins on the day immediately following delivery. The tenant is entitled to the full three calendar days to surrender possession. If a notice is served on a Tuesday, day one is Wednesday, day two is Thursday, and day three is Friday. The landlord cannot file an eviction suit until the morning of Saturday at the earliest, assuming the Justice Court accepts weekend electronic filings.

Texas Property Code Section 24.005(f) establishes four lawful mechanisms for delivering a notice to vacate. First, the notice may be handed in person to the tenant or any individual residing at the premises who is at least 16 years of age. Second, the notice may be posted by affixing it to the inside of the tenant's main entry door. Entering the residence to attach the notice to the interior side of the door requires lawful right of entry under the lease agreement.

Third, the notice may be sent via regular mail, registered mail, or certified mail with return receipt requested. If served by mail, Texas Property Code Section 24.005(f) dictates that the notice period is calculated from the date the notice is deposited in the United States mail, not the date of receipt by the tenant. However, best legal practice requires adding additional days to allow for postal transit to avoid disputes over actual notice in Justice Court.

The fourth method, outside door posting under Section 24.005(f-1), is heavily restricted. A landlord may only post notice on the outside of the main entry door if the property lacks a mailbox or mail slot, possesses a keyless bolting device that prevents entry, or has a dangerous animal that prevents entry. In addition, the notice must be placed in a sealed envelope marked in bold letters with the tenant's name and words WARNING: IMPORTANT DOCUMENT, affixed to the top of the door, and accompanied by a duplicate copy deposited in the mail before 5:00 PM on the same day in the same county.

Tenants vacating pursuant to a notice must ensure they provide a written forwarding address to preserve their security deposit return rights under Texas Property Code Section 92.107. Tenants should review our Texas security deposit demand guide to understand how landlords must account for deposits within 30 days of surrender.

Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

The most prevalent fatal mistake under Texas law is improper outside door posting under Section 24.005(f-1). Landlords frequently tape an uncovered notice to the exterior door or slip it beneath the door without mailing a companion copy by 5:00 PM that same day. If raised at trial, the Justice Court must dismiss the suit for lack of statutory notice, forcing the landlord to restart the procedure.

The second critical drafting trap is premature filing in Justice Court. If a landlord serves a three-day notice on Friday and files on Monday morning, the suit is premature. Day one was Saturday, day two Sunday, and day three Monday. The tenant possessed all of Monday to vacate. Premature filing deprives the court of jurisdiction and results in mandatory dismissal.

A third fatal error is drafting an ambiguous or conditional notice. A notice that states Pay your rent or we may file eviction does not satisfy Section 24.005. The notice must explicitly state that the tenant right of possession is terminated and demand complete surrender of the premises by the stated date.

Fourth, omitting adult cotenants from the notice to vacate creates enforcement barriers. Under Texas law, while a notice addressed to the primary tenant may suffice against unknown occupants, best practice requires naming all adults listed on the lease. If an adult leaseholder is omitted, the writ of possession issued by the Justice Court cannot be executed against that unserved occupant by the county constable.

Finally, accepting rent after serving a notice to vacate without a signed non-waiver agreement revokes the notice under Texas common law. Accepting payment for continued occupancy affirms the tenancy, defeating the pending eviction action and requiring a fresh notice.
