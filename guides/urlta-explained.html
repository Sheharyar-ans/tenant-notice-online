URLTA Explained: The Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act

Direct Legal Takeaway: URLTA is a model statute, not a law that applies on its own. It was drafted in 1972 so states could copy it into their own landlord and tenant codes. Twenty-one states enacted a version of it, and each one changed parts of it. The rules that bind you are always your own state's version, never the model text.

What URLTA Is and Where It Came From

The Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, shortened to URLTA, was drafted by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws, now called the Uniform Law Commission. It was approved at the Commission's 1972 annual conference in San Francisco, with amendments approved in 1974, and the American Bar Association endorsed it for enactment in all states.

The problem it set out to fix was fragmentation. Landlord and tenant law in the United States grew out of old English property law, where a lease was treated as a transfer of land and the promises in it were largely independent of each other. URLTA rebuilt the relationship on contract principles instead: the landlord's duties and the tenant's duties depend on each other, both sides owe good faith, and a court can refuse to enforce a rental clause that is unconscionable. Its stated purposes are to simplify and modernize the law governing the rental of homes, to encourage landlords and tenants to maintain and improve housing quality, and to make that law uniform among the states that enact it.

Because it is a model act, URLTA has no force anywhere until a state legislature passes its own version. That single point explains most of the confusion around it. When a lease, a court form or a state website refers to URLTA rules, it is referring to that state's enacted statute, which may follow the model closely or depart from it on important details.

The 2015 Revised Act (RUURLTA)

The Uniform Law Commission returned to the subject and approved a Revised Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act in July 2015. The revision updates the 1972 text for modern practice, covering topics the original handled lightly or not at all, including security deposit administration, domestic violence protections that let a victim end a lease early, and the handling of a tenant's property after a tenancy ends. The revised act has been introduced in state legislatures, including Georgia and Kentucky in 2024, but the landlord and tenant statutes in force today are still the individual state codes, most of them built on the 1972 model. If you are checking a rule, check your state's current statute rather than either model text.

Two cautions about this list. First, enacting URLTA never meant adopting it word for word. States struck sections, changed deadlines and added local rules, so two URLTA states can give different answers to the same question. Second, some states apply their version unevenly. Kentucky's act, for example, takes effect only in local governments that have adopted it under KRS 383.500, so a Kentucky reader has to confirm that the act applies in their city or county at all. Many states that never enacted URLTA still borrowed individual provisions from it, which is why its ideas show up in landlord and tenant codes well beyond these 21 states.

Security Deposits Under Section 2.101

Section 2.101 of the model act does three things. It caps the security a landlord may demand or receive, however it is labeled; the uniform text sets the ceiling by reference to one month's periodic rent, and several states adjusted that figure when they enacted it. It then controls what happens at the end of the tenancy: the landlord may apply the deposit to accrued rent and to damages caused by the tenant's noncompliance, but must deliver a written itemization of those deductions, together with the balance due, within 14 days after the tenancy terminates, the tenant gives up possession, and the tenant demands the return. Finally, it gives the rule teeth. A landlord who fails to itemize and return the money on time, or who wrongfully withholds prepaid rent, can be liable for the amount due plus damages of up to twice the amount wrongfully withheld and reasonable attorney's fees.

Deposit deadlines are the single most litigated detail in this area, and states changed them freely. Before relying on the 14-day model figure, confirm your state's return deadline and penalty in its own statute, and see our Security Deposit Return Rights Guide and the Security Deposit Demand Generator if a deposit is already being withheld.

Landlord Entry and the Access Notice Rule in Section 3.103

Section 3.103 balances the landlord's need to inspect and repair against the tenant's right to be left alone. The tenant must not unreasonably refuse entry for inspections, agreed repairs and services, or showings to prospective buyers and tenants. The landlord, in turn, may enter without consent only in a genuine emergency. Entry must not be abused or used to harass the tenant, and except in an emergency, or where notice is impracticable, the landlord must give the tenant at least 2 days' notice of the intent to enter and may enter only at reasonable times. Outside those rules, the landlord has no right of access except by court order, after abandonment, or in the narrow situations the act lists.

State versions shorten or lengthen that 2-day figure, and many states outside URLTA set their own entry notice, commonly 24 or 48 hours. Our 24-Hour Notice to Enter generator is built for the states that use a 24-hour rule; match the notice period to your state before you serve anything.

Termination Notices Under Section 4.201

Section 4.201 is the model act's notice engine, and it draws a sharp line between unpaid rent and other breaches:

  • Nonpayment of rent: If rent is unpaid when due and the tenant does not pay within 14 days after the landlord's written notice of nonpayment and of the landlord's intention to terminate, the landlord may end the rental agreement. Paying in full inside that window saves the tenancy.
  • Other material breaches: For a material failure to follow the rental agreement, or a failure to maintain the unit that affects health and safety, the landlord must give written notice describing the breach and a termination date no less than 30 days after the tenant receives it. If the tenant fixes the breach within 14 days, the agreement does not terminate. If substantially the same breach happens again within 6 months, the landlord may terminate on at least 14 days' written notice, with no second chance to cure.

These model periods are longer than what many states actually use. A state that enacted URLTA may have kept the structure but cut the days, and non-URLTA states run their own schemes entirely, such as the 3-day pay or quit notice used in states like California and Florida. If you are serving a notice today, build it from your state's current statute: start with our 3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit or 30-Day Notice to Vacate, and read the full sequence in our US Eviction Process Guide before filing anything.

Retaliation Is Prohibited by Section 5.101

Article V of the model act, a single section numbered 5.101, forbids a landlord from retaliating against a tenant by raising the rent, reducing services, or bringing or threatening an action for possession after the tenant has done any of three protected things: complained to a government agency responsible for building or housing codes about a violation affecting health and safety, complained to the landlord about a failure to maintain the premises, or organized or joined a tenants' union or similar organization. A tenant facing a retaliatory possession action can use the landlord's conduct as a defense and pursue the act's remedies for unlawful ouster. To make the protection usable, the act adds an evidentiary rule: proof that the tenant complained within 1 year before the alleged retaliation creates a presumption that the landlord acted in retaliation, unless the complaint came after the tenant received notice of a proposed rent increase or reduction in services.

Retaliation windows and remedies are another detail states rewrote on enactment, so the 1-year presumption in the model text may be shorter, longer or absent in your state. The underlying principle, that a tenant cannot be punished for reporting code violations or organizing, is one of URLTA's most widely copied ideas.

How to Use URLTA Without Being Misled by It

Treat the model act as a map, not the territory. It tells you which questions your state statute probably answers: how much deposit can be held and when it must come back, how much warning a landlord needs before entering, how a termination notice must be written and how long the cure period runs, and what counts as retaliation. The binding answers sit in your state's enacted code, in the county and city ordinances that sometimes add to it, and in the current court forms for your jurisdiction. Read the model text to understand the structure, then verify every number against your state before you act on it.
