The US Residential Eviction Process: Statutory Notice Rules & Procedural Timelines

Direct Legal Takeaway: A landlord cannot legally evict a residential tenant in the United States without serving an exact, state-sanctioned statutory notice, waiting out the full cure period, filing an unlawful detainer lawsuit, and obtaining a judicial writ of possession. Self-help lockouts are strictly illegal in all 50 states.

Phase 1: Serving the Mandatory Pre-Filing Notice

The eviction process never begins in the courthouse. It begins with the formal service of a statutory notice to vacate. Under state law, serving the correct notice is a jurisdictional prerequisite. If the notice contains mathematical errors, miscalculates non-judicial days, or demands fees outside the definition of base rent, the court must dismiss the action with prejudice.

Depending on the underlying basis for possession, landlords must deploy one of three primary instruments:

  • Nonpayment of Rent: Requires a formal demand such as the 3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit. In states such as California under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2) and Florida under Florida Statutes Section 83.56(3), weekends and judicial holidays are excluded from the 3-day computation.
  • No-Fault Tenancy Termination: Used for month-to-month leases via the 30-Day Notice to Vacate. California Civil Code Section 1946.1 mandates 60 days for tenancies exceeding one year, while New York Real Property Law Section 226-c requires up to 90 days for tenancies over two years.
  • Landlord Entry and Inspections: Prior to assessing property conditions, landlords must provide written advance notice utilizing the 24-Hour Notice to Enter in compliance with California Civil Code Section 1954 and privacy standards.

Phase 2: Service of Process Standards

Merely writing or emailing a notice is generally legally ineffective. State civil procedure codes mandate strict methods of delivery:

  1. Personal In-Hand Delivery: Handing the physical notice directly to the named tenant.
  2. Substituted Service: Handing the notice to a competent person of suitable age and discretion residing at the unit, accompanied by a contemporaneous first-class mailing.
  3. Nail and Mail (Posting): Affixing the notice conspicuously to the front entrance of the property and sending a duplicate copy by certified mail where expressly permitted by statute.

Phase 3: Filing the Unlawful Detainer Summons and Complaint

Once the statutory notice period expires without cure or voluntary surrender, the landlord gains the right to file an Unlawful Detainer (eviction) action in the appropriate county municipal or justice court. The tenant must be served with a court summons and given an opportunity to file an Answer detailing statutory defenses.

Phase 4: Common Tenant Defenses and Dismissals

Tenants routinely defeat defective eviction filings by proving one of the following legal defenses:

  • Breach of Warranty of Habitability: The landlord failed to maintain basic heating, plumbing, or waterproofing standards.
  • Retaliatory Eviction: The notice was served within 180 days of the tenant organizing a tenants union or reporting building code violations to local health inspectors.
  • Improper Accounting of Deposits: Landlords who unlawfully withhold funds may face counterclaims under our Security Deposit Return Rights Guide and our dedicated Security Deposit Demand Generator.

Phase 5: Judicial Judgment and Sheriff Execution

If the court rules in favor of the property owner, the judge issues a Judgment for Possession. The clerk then issues a formal Writ of Possession to the county sheriff or marshal. Only a sworn law enforcement officer has the legal authority to execute a lockout and physically restore possession to the owner.
