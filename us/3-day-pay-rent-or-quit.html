California 3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit: CCP § 1161(2)

Under California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2) and Florida Statute 83.56, a 3-day notice demands delinquent rent or property surrender. The day of service, Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays are strictly excluded from the three-day computation before an unlawful detainer can be filed.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing Act

In the United States, summary eviction for nonpayment of rent is strictly conditioned on service of a compliant statutory notice. In California, rent demands are governed by the California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2). This enactment authorizes an unlawful detainer action only after the tenant has defaulted in the payment of rent pursuant to the lease agreement, and three days notice in writing requiring its payment or possession of the property has been served upon the tenant. Using a 3 day notice to pay rent or quit template free tool ensures that property managers accurately adhere to these jurisdictional mandates.

In Florida, nonpayment demands operate under Florida Statute Section 83.56(3). The landlord is required to deliver a written demand granting three business days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal court holidays, before terminating the rental agreement or commencing court eviction. In Texas, nonpayment procedure is governed by Texas Property Code Section 24.005. Texas statutes require a landlord to give a tenant at least three days written notice to vacate before filing a forcible detainer suit, unless the parties agreed in a written lease to a shorter or longer notice duration.

Crucially, under California law following amendments to CCP Section 1161(2), the calculation of the three-day cure period strictly excludes Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays recognized by the California court system. A landlord who files an unlawful detainer summons and complaint before the full expiration of three clear judicial business days lacks standing, resulting in mandatory court dismissal.

Legal Service of Process: How to Deliver This Notice

Defective service of a 3-day notice is a fatal jurisdictional defect. In an unlawful detainer trial, the landlord bears the strict burden of proving service in accordance with state civil procedure. Under California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1162 and Florida Statutes Section 83.56(4), service must be executed through one of three statutory methods:

1. Personal Delivery

Delivering the notice directly into the hands of the tenant. This provides unassailable proof of actual delivery and starts the statutory clock on the following judicial business day.

2. Substituted Service

Leaving a copy with a person of suitable age and discretion at the tenant residence or usual place of business, and mailing a second copy via first-class mail addressed to the tenant.

3. Posting & Mailing

Affixing the notice conspicuously upon the primary entrance of the rental property and mailing a duplicate copy on the same date where personal service could not be accomplished.

Fatal Drafting Mistakes That Invalidate This Notice

Eviction proceedings are strict summary matters. Even nominal technical errors invalidate the entire lawsuit:

  • Overstating the Overdue Rent: Demanding even one dollar more than the lawfully accrued base rent renders the 3-day notice fatal in California. Under California law, late fees, bounced check charges, interest, and unpaid utility bills cannot be combined into a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit.
  • Miscalculating Judicial Holidays: Counting a Saturday, Sunday, or court holiday as one of the three statutory days invalidates any subsequent eviction filing. Day one begins on the first judicial business day following completed physical delivery.
  • Omitting Payee Availability Information: Under California CCP Section 1161(2), failing to state the payee name, physical remittance address, phone number, and hours of availability between 9:00 AM and 5:00 PM on weekdays constitutes a fatal defect.
  • Accepting Partial Payment Without Non-Waiver: Accepting a partial rent check without executing an explicit, signed written reservation of rights waives the notice, resetting the landlord right to file for eviction.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the 3-day notice period calculated under California CCP § 1161(2)?

California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2) expressly excludes Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays from the 3-day computation. The day of service is excluded, and Day 1 begins on the next judicial business day.

Can late fees or utilities be included in a 3-day notice to pay rent?

No. Under California law, demanding late fees, utility surcharges, or interest in a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit fatalizes the notice, making any subsequent unlawful detainer lawsuit subject to immediate dismissal.

What are the rules for serving a 3 day notice to pay rent or quit under Florida Statute 83.56?

Florida Statute Section 83.56(3) mandates three business days excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal court holidays. The notice must demand payment of rent or delivery of possession of the premises directly to the landlord.

Does Texas law provide a statutory cure period for unpaid rent?

Under Texas Property Code Section 24.005, a landlord must provide at least three days written notice to vacate before filing a forcible detainer suit, unless a written lease specifies a different notice period such as 24 hours.

What payment instructions must be specified on a California 3-day rent demand?

The notice must specify the exact dollar amount of overdue rent, the name, telephone number, physical address of the payee, and the ordinary business hours during which payment can be personally remitted.

What happens if a landlord accepts partial rent after serving a 3-day notice?

Accepting partial payment without a contemporaneous written non-waiver agreement waives the notice to pay rent or quit as a matter of law, requiring the landlord to issue a brand new notice for any remaining unpaid balance.
