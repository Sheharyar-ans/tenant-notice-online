California 3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit: CCP § 1161(2)

Direct Legal Rule: California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2) requires landlords to give tenants a written three-day notice demanding exact delinquent rent or possession of the property. The three-day cure period strictly excludes Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays. Non-rent charges must be omitted.

Statutory Legal Foundation & Governing State Code

In California residential tenancy practice, the 3-day notice to pay rent or quit is governed by California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2). When a tenant continues in possession of real property after a default in the payment of rent pursuant to the lease or agreement under which the property is held, the landlord must serve a 3-day notice in writing requiring its payment, stating the amount which is due, or possession of the property.

Crucially, under legislative amendments enacted via Assembly Bill 2343, Saturdays, Sundays, and other judicial holidays are strictly excluded from the calculation of the three-day notice period. This protective reform ensures that residential tenants have three full business days to obtain banking instruments, secure emergency financial assistance, or transmit electronic funds before facing the forfeiture of their tenancy.

In Florida, residential nonpayment notices are codified under Florida Statutes Section 83.56(3). The landlord must provide written demand for payment of the rent or possession of the premises within 3 days, similarly excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays observed by the county court clerk. In Texas, Texas Property Code Section 24.005 establishes a default 3-day notice to vacate for unpaid rent unless a written lease agreement explicitly allows a different notice timeframe (such as 24 hours).

Judicial Day-Counting Rules: Exclusion of Weekends and Court Holidays

A substantial percentage of eviction actions fail during judicial review due to basic errors in calendar day counting. Under California Code of Civil Procedure Section 12, time within which any act provided by law is to be done is computed by excluding the first day and including the last.

  • The Day of Service is Excluded: If a 3-day notice is personally served on a Thursday, Thursday does not count as Day 1.
  • First Business Day is Friday (Day 1): Friday constitutes the initial statutory cure day.
  • Saturday and Sunday are Excluded: Neither Saturday nor Sunday can be counted toward the 3-day cure window.
  • Monday is Day 2: Provided Monday is not an official state or court holiday.
  • Tuesday is Day 3: The tenant has until the close of business on Tuesday to tender the full rent balance.
  • First Lawful Filing Date is Wednesday: The landlord cannot file an unlawful detainer complaint until Wednesday morning. Filing prematurely on Tuesday deprives the court of jurisdiction and results in automatic dismissal.

Service of Process: How to Legally Deliver This Demand

A 3-day notice to pay rent or quit must be served in exact compliance with California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1162 (or corresponding state rules in Florida and Texas). Acceptable statutory methods include:

Personal In-Hand Service

Delivering a physical copy directly to the tenant personally wherever they may be located.

Substituted Service

Leaving a copy with a person of suitable age and discretion at the residence, followed by immediate first-class mailing.

Posting and Mailing

Affixing the notice conspicuously to the front door and mailing a duplicate copy if personal service fails after reasonable diligence.

Common Pitfalls That Void This Rent Demand

  • Inflating the Demand with Non-Rent Sums: Combining late fees, utility surcharges, pet rent penalties, or administrative fees into the demand amount violates California CCP Section 1161(2). The notice must demand rent alone.
  • Omission of Mandatory Payment Instructions: Under California law, failure to list the authorized payee's name, telephone number, physical street address, and operating hours invalidates the notice on its face.
  • Refusing Full Tender During the Notice Period: If a tenant delivers full payment of the demanded sum prior to the expiration of the 3 days, the landlord is legally required to accept it. Refusal constitutes an absolute defense to eviction.
  • Demanding Rent Stale by More Than One Year: California law prohibits demanding overdue rent that accrued more than one year prior to the date of service in a summary 3-day notice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the 3-day notice period calculated under California CCP § 1161(2)?

California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161(2) expressly excludes Saturdays, Sundays, and judicial holidays from the 3-day computation. The day of service is excluded, and Day 1 begins on the next business day.

Can late fees or utilities be included in a 3-day notice to pay rent?

No. Under California law, demanding late fees, utility surcharges, or interest in a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit fatalizes the notice, making any subsequent unlawful detainer lawsuit subject to immediate dismissal.

What information must be included regarding rent payment details in California?

The notice must specify the exact delinquent balance, the name and telephone number of the person to whom rent must be delivered, physical payment address, and the days and hours the person is available to accept payment.

How does Florida Statutes § 83.56(3) govern nonpayment rent notices?

Florida Statutes Section 83.56(3) requires a 3-day written demand excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. The notice must specify the exact amount due and advise the tenant that failure to pay or vacate will lead to eviction proceedings.

What happens if a tenant tenders the full rent within the 3-day statutory window?

If the tenant tenders the full delinquent rent demanded in the notice within the statutory cure window, the landlord is legally obligated to accept the payment, and the default is completely cured.
